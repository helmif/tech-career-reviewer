# Product design evaluation

Apply this reference to product design, UX, UI, interaction design, UX research, and design leadership targets.

## Strong signals

- A coherent case-study narrative from problem and research through decisions, iterations, outcome, and reflection.
- Clear personal contribution within a cross-functional process.
- Design rationale tied to user needs, constraints, evidence, and tradeoffs.
- Appropriate craft evidence in interaction, information architecture, visual hierarchy, prototyping, research, accessibility, and systems thinking.
- Outcomes such as task success, comprehension, adoption, conversion, accessibility, support load, or validated learning.
- Iteration evidence, including what failed or changed.
- Design-system contributions described through adoption, consistency, accessibility, or team leverage.

Polished screens without reasoning are insufficient for most product design targets.

## Seniority calibration

- **Entry or junior:** demonstrates foundations, thoughtful process, iteration, collaboration, and clear craft growth.
- **Mid-level:** independently owns end-to-end design for a problem area and makes evidence-based tradeoffs.
- **Senior:** leads ambiguous problems, influences product direction, improves systems, and raises team quality.
- **Lead, principal, or head:** shapes multi-team design strategy, operating mechanisms, talent, systems, and organization-level outcomes.

## Common weak signals

- Portfolio dominated by final visuals with no problem, constraints, or decisions.
- Generic process diagrams repeated across every case study.
- Unclear ownership in team projects.
- Research activities listed without findings or decisions.
- Confidential work shown without permission or adequate sanitization.
- Design-system component counts without adoption or product impact.

## Evidence questions

1. What user problem and behavior were you trying to change?
2. Which research or product evidence shaped the design?
3. What did you personally own?
4. What constraints and tradeoffs changed the solution?
5. What alternatives were explored, tested, or rejected?
6. What outcome or learning followed, and how was it measured?
7. What would you change now, and why?

## Proof recommendations

Prefer two or three deep, relevant case studies over a large gallery. Each should establish context, contribution, evidence, decisions, iterations, outcome, and reflection. Sanitize internal data and use a password-protected presentation when confidentiality requires it.
