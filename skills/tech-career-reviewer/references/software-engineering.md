# Software engineering evaluation

Apply this reference to backend, frontend, mobile, platform, infrastructure, data, security, embedded, and full-stack engineering targets.

## Strong signals

- Clear personal ownership of a component, service, architecture decision, migration, incident, or delivery outcome.
- Technical decisions connected to requirements, constraints, alternatives, and tradeoffs.
- Appropriate depth in reliability, performance, security, maintainability, testing, accessibility, or developer experience.
- Scale described with relevant units: traffic, data, users, services, latency, availability, deployments, cost, or team reach.
- Production learning: incidents, observability, gradual rollout, migrations, reversibility, or operational responsibility.
- Collaboration that reveals engineering influence rather than vague teamwork.
- Repositories or artifacts with usable documentation and evidence of engineering judgment.

Technology lists are supporting context, not a substitute for engineering evidence.

## Seniority calibration

- **Entry or junior:** implemented scoped features, wrote tests, learned systems, debugged effectively, and collaborated reliably.
- **Mid-level:** independently delivered production work, owned components, handled tradeoffs, and improved quality or operations.
- **Senior:** led ambiguous or high-risk work, influenced architecture, reduced systemic risk, mentored others, and improved team outcomes.
- **Staff or principal:** shaped multi-team technical direction, created leverage, aligned architecture with business constraints, and delivered durable organization-level outcomes.

## Common weak signals

- "Built APIs" or "worked on microservices" with no problem, ownership, scale, or result.
- Senior titles supported only by tool breadth or years.
- Architecture vocabulary without decisions or tradeoffs.
- Team achievements written as sole authorship.
- GitHub activity presented as proof of production impact.
- Unprioritized stacks containing every tool encountered.

## Evidence questions

Choose only the questions that could materially change the review:

1. What component or decision did you personally own?
2. What requirement or failure made the work necessary?
3. What scale or operational constraints mattered?
4. Which alternatives did you consider, and why did you choose this approach?
5. What changed in reliability, latency, cost, delivery speed, adoption, or support load?
6. How was the change tested, deployed, observed, and rolled back?
7. Who else was involved, and how did you influence the result?

## Proof recommendations

Prefer an architecture note, decision record, project README, test strategy, benchmark, incident learning summary, migration narrative, or focused open-source contribution. Remove confidential system details and employer data.
